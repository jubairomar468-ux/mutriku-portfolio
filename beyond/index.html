01 / TECHNOLOGY AND PLACE

Waves move water.
Moving water moves air.

Mutriku’s oscillating water columns are integrated into its harbour breakwater. The official test-site specification gives a total installed capacity of 296 kW and grid operation since 2011. It also provides facilities for testing air turbines, controls and auxiliary equipment.[19]

Why the breakwater matters

The shared structure makes the project distinctive: power generation and harbour functions share physical infrastructure. BiMEP lists road access, a 750 mm chamber-to-turbine opening and a connection through a 460 V/13.2 kV transformer.[19] For a new investment decision, the useful comparison is the additional equipment and works required for wave power, together with a clearly stated allocation of shared infrastructure.

Could a different turbine improve performance?

Gato and colleagues tested a 30 kW biradial turbo-generation prototype. Their reported mean time-averaged peak efficiency was 37% higher than that of the Wells turbines during the trials.[4] This is a relative improvement in a particular efficiency metric. It does not demonstrate 37% more annual electricity for the whole plant.

Technology comparison and the next question to resolve
OptionEvidence availableDecision still needed
Wells turbineInstalled technology with operating experience.How much output is limited by control, availability and turbine matching?
Biradial turbineSea-trial efficiency improvement in the reported metric [4].Does annual net output justify additional manufacture, maintenance and replacement?

Proposed evaluation: compare matched sea states, parasitic loads, downtime and component life before extrapolating a turbine result to a plant-wide sustainability claim.

Above the water does not mean free from salt

The rotor is driven by air, but marine exposure still matters. The 2025 control study discusses salt-related equipment failures and corrosion or splashing risks at high water levels.[15] Maintenance and replacement burdens therefore remain part of the life-cycle assessment.

02 / PERFORMANCE IN CONTEXT

Capacity is a rating.
Output is an observation.

The 2014–2016 study reports mean annual generation of 246,468.7 kWh, with approximately ten turbines working on average in the 14-turbine configuration analysed.[2] The original 16-position design is a different denominator; neither figure establishes today’s availability.

The 600,000 kWh/year forecast was published in 2012 with expectations of further tuning and automatic operation. The same statement records the December 2011 storm shutdown and resumption in March 2012.[3] The measured 2014–2016 mean was 41.1% of that earlier forecast. This ratio is neither capacity factor nor turbine efficiency.

EVE also reported annual production of approximately 300 MWh in that announcement.[13] EVE gives no precise averaging window for that estimate. Its 2023 annual report gives a calendar-year observation of 266 MWh in 2023 and confirms cumulative generation above 3 GWh by the end of that year.[20]

How long does the comparison hold?

The measured 2014–2016 mean and the 2023 annual total both fell below 600 MWh/year. The cumulative milestone gives the scale of generation across the intervening period. Annual records are needed to establish performance in any other individual year. A current comparison needs annual net export, downtime and equivalent sea-state data.

Power is measured in kW; energy in kWh, MWh or GWh. No annual time series has been inferred from the three milestones.

03 / LIFE-CYCLE ACCOUNTING

The footprint begins
before generation.

The 2026 LCA reports 226 g CO₂e/kWh for a 20-year model with 4.93 GWh of generation. Rounded contributions are 195 from construction, 12.4 from operation and maintenance and 17.9 from end of life.[1]

The boundary includes breakwater construction but excludes its maintenance. Dismantling emissions were set to zero because data were unavailable. Operating inventory came from plant staff; construction and end-of-life inputs used secondary information.[1]

Two decisions need different boundaries

Assess the complete installation

Account for the shared structure and all relevant maintenance and retirement activities. Explain how burdens are distributed between harbour protection and electricity.

Assess an incremental upgrade

Compare the additional materials and works with additional lifetime electricity. Do not treat an existing structure as impact-free when making claims about the whole plant.

The two comparisons above are proposed decision frameworks. Neither is a new completed LCA. Missing processes require an uncertainty range or additional inventory data before a robust numerical comparison.

Carbon does not describe every impact

The LCA also considers resource depletion and toxicity-related indicators.[1] These modelled indicators should be considered alongside site monitoring. A small carbon contribution cannot by itself establish low habitat disturbance or community acceptance.

Proposed material strategy: compare lower-impact supply options only after checking marine durability. An initially lower footprint could be offset by more frequent repairs or early replacement. Record the replacement assumptions and test them over the same service period.

Materials behind the construction footprint

The inventory includes approximately 12.9 kt of concrete and 21.3 kt of limestone. The paper also reports construction as the principal contributor to most of its other impact categories.[1] These are model inventory and impact results, not measurements of ecological damage at Mutriku.

The poster’s 53–94% higher greenhouse-gas emissions statement reproduces the comparison reported in the LCA’s discussion of Figure 7.[1] The paper itself identifies differences in material inventories, design, output and assumptions. Treat this as that paper’s comparison, not a harmonised ranking of all ocean-energy technologies or a new calculation by this website.

Retirement needs a complete inventory

Steel recycling can reduce metal-depletion indicators in the model, while other categories respond differently. The paper’s prose and Table 4 do not consistently identify the same highest-toxicity recycling scenario. The defensible conclusion is category dependence; this companion does not select a universal winning recycling rate.[1]

Dismantling set to zero means missing inventory data. It does not establish zero removal emissions or zero habitat effects. Compare feasible reuse, removal, recycling and disposal routes before claiming an end-of-life benefit.

04 / TEST THE ASSUMPTIONS

When would the energy
investment be recovered?

The LCA offers a preliminary energy-payback estimate of 26.2 years. This exceeds its assumed 20-year service life.[1] The tool below explores the arithmetic; it does not predict future plant performance.

Energy-payback explorer

Change one assumption at a time, then examine combined scenarios.

26.2 years

Energy payback exceeds the selected service life in this illustrative model.

31.0% more annual output than the baseline is needed to reach payback by year 20.

Energy payback = 26.2 × embodied-energy factor ÷ annual-output factor

At unchanged embodied energy, 26.2 ÷ 20 = 1.31. The resulting 31% threshold is a scaling exercise. Extending life may require additional equipment or repair energy, so the embodied-energy control should also change when assessing such a scenario.

Interpretation: service life changes the deadline for recovery; it does not automatically reduce the energy invested. No claim about economic payback or avoided carbon follows from this calculation alone. Those require separate cost and displaced-electricity assumptions.

How to read the poster’s solar and wind comparison

The 2026 LCA quotes eight years for solar PV and 1.5 years for wind from Aman et al. (2015).[1] These are secondary, historical comparison values. They are not current technology-wide benchmarks and do not share a demonstrated common boundary with this Mutriku model. They are therefore not used to calibrate the calculator or rank present-day alternatives.

05 / ENVIRONMENT AND SOCIETY

Who benefits?
What bears the burden?

The categories below follow the poster’s construction, operation and retirement stages. “Modelled” identifies LCA outputs; “reported” identifies a source’s observation or account; “unresolved” identifies a gap. A proposed response is not a demonstrated benefit.

Environmental impacts

Modelled burdenConstruction: materials and climate

195 g CO₂e/kWh is the construction contribution in the published model. Concrete and limestone dominate the material quantities [1]. Habitat effects need separate evidence.

Modelled contributionOperation and maintenance: 12.4 g CO₂e/kWh

This stage is about 5.5% of the rounded-stage total [1]. It cannot stand for the full 226 g CO₂e/kWh footprint or establish low ecological impact.

Unresolved local effectsOperation: sound, habitat and flow

The reported acoustic findings have sampling limits [5, 10, 18]. The OWC addition and the complete breakwater require separate environmental boundaries.

Potential benefit and burdenRetirement: recovery and removal

Recycling has category-dependent effects [1]. Removal-related habitat disturbance is unassessed here; missing dismantling emissions were assigned zero in the LCA.

Social impacts

Reported concernConstruction: noise and disruption

The developer-supplied record describes storm-time construction noise heard 3–10 km away and local concern [10]. This is a reported audibility distance, not a measured harm radius.

Source-reported activityOperation: electricity, testing and visits

The site generates electricity and hosts turbine trials [4, 9, 13]. The 2012 statement records visitor interest [3], and the Basque Coast Geopark lists a guided visit [21]. Quantifying local income or employment would require visitor and spending data.

Historical public investment€6.7M project; €2.3M energy installation

The July 2011 government announcement gives these capital costs [16]. They are not annual costs, a current valuation or a levelised cost of electricity.

Unresolved distributionParticipation and retirement responsibility

Current acceptance and employment effects remain unverified. The 2013 study informs participation [6]; retirement funding, worker impacts and port disruption need their own assessment.

These reports are not counted as independent replications simply because they have different publication dates. None establishes ecological safety at every distance, in every sea state or for every species.[5][10][18]

Breakwater, habitat and the incremental OWC

Tethys describes a hard-bottom coastal setting and reports no significant additional sediment or flow change from the OWC extension.[10] WESE describes the plant as operating on the breakwater’s artificial substrate and focuses its seafloor findings on other devices.[12] The natural site description and the built substrate refer to different things; neither demonstrates an absence of whole-breakwater impacts. Seafloor and fish results from other test sites should not be relabelled as Mutriku results.

No complaints is a limited social indicator

The Tethys record reports no complaints about the construction-noise issue during the following ten years.[10] This is not a representative acceptance survey. The longer, 13-year figure attributed to Interreg [14] could not be independently confirmed. The documented ten-year period is the basis used here.

The 2013 study supports meaningful involvement, while the later SafeWAVE work adds engagement and monitoring context.[6][5] The newer work is not a directly comparable repeat survey of the same community.

Proposed approach: involve residents, port users and workers in monitoring priorities, publish responses to concerns and explain which decisions changed. Include people who may not use a formal complaints channel.

07 / CRITICAL EVALUATION

Match the claim
to the evidence.

This focused case-study review prioritises academic evidence published from 2018 onward on life-cycle impacts, operation, turbines and environmental monitoring. Older work supplies historical or site-specific evidence and is considered alongside later findings. Operator and government records provide dated context. Access levels and any unresolved retrieval limits are stated beside each reference. References [1]–[18] follow the poster. The companion adds the operator specification [19], EVE’s 2023 annual report [20] and the Geopark tour listing [21].

How older studies are used

Publication date alone does not determine whether a finding remains useful. Older studies may support a claim when later evidence has not superseded or refuted it, or when no suitable newer study addresses the same question. That judgement must remain claim-specific: absence of a replacement does not establish that historical conditions still apply. Historical forecasts and opening costs retain their original dates rather than being replaced with current values. Technical claims use later work where available, including the 2025 control study [15]. The 2013 Mutriku study [6] informs the discussion of participation; it does not measure current acceptance. Later SafeWAVE research [5] provides additional monitoring and engagement context, without being treated as a directly comparable repeat of the earlier study. This focused review does not claim to have identified every later publication.

Why the website keeps “unknown” visible

Missing measurements cannot be replaced with a favourable assumption. An unresolved effect is also different from a demonstrated harm. Keeping the distinction visible directs the next study towards the information that could change the decision.

Why there is no universal ranking against wind or solar

A credible comparison needs compatible boundaries, locations, lifetimes and electricity functions. This companion does not combine unmatched headline values into a league table. Equivalent alternatives should be assessed for the actual decision and setting.

How the numbers and visuals were prepared

Charts were redrawn from the cited values. The forecast ratio, rounded stage share and payback sensitivity are transparent arithmetic. The OWC and acoustic figures explain mechanisms and evidence scope; they are not new experimental results. The chart-data CSV includes units, dates, source numbers and calculation notes. The source catalogue merges the supplied reference workbook with the earlier companion sources, and the claim-check CSV records the treatment of all 57 workbook claims.

Overall judgement

Mutriku demonstrates useful generation and a setting for technology development. A defensible improvement case must show additional lifetime value while accounting for materials, maintenance and retirement. Environmental monitoring and meaningful participation are necessary to judge how benefits and burdens are distributed.

The next decision should follow comparable evidence, with uncertainty carried into the assessment.
